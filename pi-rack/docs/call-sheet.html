Start here

The best hour has no Raspberry Pi in it

Three allocation strategies were designed independently — one to reduce loss, one to maximise value, one to minimise burden. All three reached this conclusion separately and then buried it under hostnames. It belongs at the top.

Four actions, about an hour, roughly thirty cents a month. None of them needs a board, a card, or the rack to exist — and doing them deletes the justification for about a third of everything else that was proposed.

  1. Cut the DVR's path out10 min · $0

    192.168.1.49 runs uc-httpd 1.0.0 — a XiongMai DVR web server with well-known unauthenticated RCE, and a standard botnet target. It sits on the flat /24 beside the NAS's open SMB and the workbench holding 4.8 TB of wedding footage. Block its outbound access on the router. Full VLAN segmentation is better and can come later; don't wait for it.

  2. Reserve the addresses that already bit you15 min · $0

    Home Assistant — 2005 entities, 44 integrations — is on a DHCP lease with no reservation, and that address has already moved once during a migration. Reserve .42, .66, .70, .75, .83, then confirm the pool doesn't overlap .100–.130.

  3. Give the HA guest the RAM it's sitting next to5 min · $0

    The haos guest has 4 GB on a 16 GB host. Several proposals existed only to relieve pressure inside that VM. Most of them evaporate at 8 GB.

  4. One verified offsite copy of the irreplaceable subset1 hr · ~$0.30/mo

    The only item here that survives the house burning down. restic has a native Windows binary — run it from Task Scheduler into a Backblaze B2 bucket with Object Lock and a key that cannot delete. Provider-enforced immutability is a stronger guarantee than an append-only flag on a board in the same house as the threat.

The blocking prerequisite nobody has done. Before any of this means anything, the tier-1 set has to be written down: walk one complete wedding job from cards to delivery and record every path it touches. If one folder is missed, it is backed up nowhere and verified by nothing — which is worse than having none of this.

It is not a big list. Probably 30–200 GB against ~49 TiB of bulk media. That ratio is the entire point.

The allocation

Four boards on, one in a box

Named for the film crew, because that is the trade this rack mostly serves.

gaffer

Deploy

Pi 4 · smallest board · SD · .101

The witness — canary + watchdog

The highest-consensus item in the exercise: every strategy proposed a version of it. Its value isn't speed, it's independence. A backup checked by the process that wrote it is not checked. An alert that travels through Home Assistant can't tell you Home Assistant is down. And HA is a VM on .66, so nothing inside HA can restart HA.

If it dies: nothing in the house changes — you just stop being told when something else breaks. An outbound heartbeat covers that.

sparks

Deploy

Pi 4 · 4 GB floor · USB SSD · .102

ESPHome build server

Not speculative — it unblocks something already logged as blocked. Three routes to rebuilding the Voice PE firmware are closed, so it currently happens in a venv on the Windows box, with the ESP-IDF cache on a drive that has under 50 GB free. The 4 GB floor isn't negotiable: an esp-idf build on 2 GB thrashes or gets OOM-killed, and presents as a mysterious hang.

If it dies: builds fall back to the workbench venv — exactly how they're done today. Capability unchanged.

runner

Deploy

Pi 4 · smallest board · SD · .103

The travelling LAN kit

Not part of the rack — it lives in the camera bag. The counter-intuitive argument for it: it's the lowest-burden board in the plan precisely because it's switched off about 360 days a year. DHCP, DNS, NTP, an info page and iperf3, fixing the four things that go wrong at every LAN before anyone has unpacked a PC.

Safety interlock: its DHCP server ships disabled and refuses to arm if it can see the home gateway.

standby

Gated

Pi 5 · boot must match .70 · .104

Warm spare for the Frigate box

.70 is the only single-purpose production machine on the LAN with no redundancy — six cameras, a Coral TPU — and a Pi 4 is not a drop-in. Deliberately not a video review host: BCM2712 has no hardware H.264 encoder at all, so every transcode would be software x264 while the workbench has Resolve.

Blocked on: two minutes of lsblk over SSH. That single fact is the difference between a spare and a label.

the fifth

Boxed

Pi 4 · labelled · on the shelf

Deliberately not deployed

Not laziness — the honest answer. A board sitting in a rack "as a spare" is a board that gets quietly repurposed in three weeks. Five always-on boards is five things that can break and page a solo operator on a wedding weekend. Its fleet.toml entry is written and disabled, so pressing it into service is one command.

Recovery time for any of the three above, using it: about twenty minutes.

Deliberately not built

The more useful list

126 ideas were generated, then attacked by a pass whose only job was to refute them. 31 were cut outright and many more were re-homed onto hardware that already exists.

Any Pi in the bulk data path

One pass over ~49 TiB through a single Pi NIC is over 120 hours. The machines that own the disks move the bytes; a Pi owns the control plane. This killed the mini-NAS, the S3 target, the store-and-forward relay and the duplicate crawler.

DNS and DHCP on a Pi

Rejected by two of three strategies. It converts "an SD card died" into "nobody in the house has internet". The role is built and works — it just isn't recommended, and it refuses to come up as a single resolver without an explicit override.

A camera-card ingest appliance

The premise was wrong: there's ~27 TB free across the other volumes. The problem is filing discipline, not capacity. And it's the one job where failure costs a non-reshootable client deliverable — the worst place for a headless self-built box.

fail2ban, log2ram, k3s, PXE netboot

With password auth off there's nothing to brute-force. Trixie already ships a volatile journal. A k3s control plane is most of a 2 GB Pi 4. Netboot would make the NAS a single point of failure for every Pi at once.

Uptime monitoring for client websites

Dental Story does social media, not web hosting. Alerts about sites you neither built nor host — at 3am, with no credentials to fix them — create an implied availability responsibility nobody is paying for.

The wizard

One file, and a gate that has been tested on your drives

fleet.toml is the source of truth. Everything written to a card — hostname, static IP, SSH key, role software — is derived from it, so nothing is typed twice.

> pi-wizard doctor
> pi-wizard flash gaffer

The step that actually turns a generic card into this node is seed — three text files on a FAT32 partition — and it deliberately needs no Administrator. So the fallback always works: write the card with the Imager GUI, then seed it.

What goes on the card was verified rather than assumed. Raspberry Pi OS trixie provisions with cloud-init, not the custom.toml mechanism nearly every current tutorial describes. This was settled by downloading the official image, checking its SHA-256, parsing its partition table and reading the cloud-init config out of its root filesystem. Building on the stale guidance produces a Pi with no user, no key and no network.

The near-miss worth knowing about. Eight of the nine data drives on the workbench report bus type USB, because they live in QNAP enclosures. The obvious safety rule for an SD-card writer — "only offer removable disks" — would have cheerfully offered the 12.7 TB volume holding 4.8 TB of wedding footage.

So the gate refuses on four independent grounds, any one of which is sufficient:

  • flagged as the system or boot disk
  • bus type is not USB, SD or MMC
  • larger than 2 TiB — no Pi boot device is
  • carries a protected volume label (M1, Video Work, M6 Backup…)

Then it makes you retype a fingerprint like 11:SDXC Card:64.0 GB, and re-checks immediately before writing — a card can be pulled and a data drive plugged into the same reader in between. A test asserts specifically that the bus check alone would have failed, so nobody later simplifies the gate down to it.

Honest caveats

What isn't proven yet

Nothing has been booted on real hardware. Every claim about what happens on first boot is derived from the image's own configuration, read out of the image itself. That is stronger than following a tutorial, but it is not the same as a Pi that came up. It won't be verified until the first card goes in.

The provisioning research had one pass, not two. Three agents assigned to independently re-verify the imaging, first-boot and SSD-boot mechanics died on a session limit before reporting. The material is source-checked, but unrepeated — the EEPROM and NVMe sections in particular should be checked against the real board before being relied on.

Five facts are still unknown, and two of them decide whether hardware gets bought. They are twenty minutes of measurement, and they are Phase 1 for exactly that reason.
